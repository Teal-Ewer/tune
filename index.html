Tuning protocol

Order matters. Skipping or reordering breaks the math.

1

Measure conditions

Temperature, both at the tuning area and at the actual performance field. Handheld thermometers are far more reliable than weather apps for spot conditions. Measure close to tuning time if conditions are shifting.

2

Compute section targets

Plug conditions into the calculator. Set Pit tuned to the nominal pitch your pit gear was tuned to at room temperature (440 or 442 Hz) and set Match brass to the pit instrument you most want the brass to lock with on the field (Average is a safe default if you're unsure). Use calibrated buffer factors if you have them, defaults otherwise. The four section targets typically spread across 1–3 Hz depending on the indoor/outdoor delta.

3

Warm up to thermal equilibrium

A few minutes of normal playing to stabilize every horn at the tuning area's conditions. A rough ensemble tune and intra-section spot-checking are fine here - players align to each other loosely so the final tune is a small adjustment, not a reset.

4

Final tune to section targets

Each section to its computed Hz. The cross-section frequencies are intentionally different - communicate this explicitly so nobody "corrects" it later.

5

No ensemble play between tuning and downbeat

Any ensemble work in this window invites by-ear adjustment that undoes the section-specific targeting. Intra-section work only.

6

Iterate calibration after the show

From recordings or live observation, note how each section sat against the pit in exposed sustained passages, ideally comparing early vs. late in the show. Refine the buffer factors in the calculator's advanced panel. Across a season the calibration converges on values that reliably predict your specific ensemble's behavior in known conditions.

Gotchas

  • Cold tuning. A horn tuned cold lands 5–15 cents sharp once warm, swamping the careful compensation. Always warm up first.
  • Stale measurements. If you tuned 30 minutes before downbeat and conditions shifted, the targets are wrong. Re-measure close to performance time when possible.
  • Uncalibrated buffer factors. The defaults are physics-informed estimates, not measurements of your horns. Until you calibrate, expect the math to be directionally right but quantitatively off.
  • Wind across the field. Wind pulls heat out of horns faster than still air, so effective ambient is lower than the thermometer reads. Expect more drift than predicted on windy days.
  • Pit bars drift opposite to brass. Wood/synthetic (marimba) and aluminum (vibes) both flatten noticeably in heat, steel (glock) barely at all - while brass rises. The Match brass to selector compensates for this. Pick the instrument that carries the most exposed lines, or leave it on Average.
  • Equilibration happens during the show, not before it. Without on-field prep, low brass will be drifting throughout the early portion of the show - trumpets reach field equilibrium in 2–3 minutes, tubas in 8–10. Pre-staging horns in shade at performance-area conditions before warm-up reduces this; the metal body pre-equilibrates while the air column adjusts during play.
  • Sun on the bell. Direct sun warms the metal and raises the effective ambient floor for the air inside. A horn in sun and another in shade behave measurably differently in extreme conditions.
  • Show length matters. A 12-minute show gives tubas time to fully equilibrate; a 4-minute exhibition doesn't. For shorter performances, low brass's effective buffer is lower than steady-state - dial tuba/contra buffers down accordingly.

The physics, step by step

The pitch a brass instrument produces is set by standing waves in its air column. For a tube of fixed length, every resonant frequency scales directly with the speed of sound in the air inside it - written c in acoustics (the standard symbol; a separate convention from the c used for the speed of light in physics):

f ∝ c

The walkthrough below uses these symbols:

f
frequency the horn produces (the pitch)
c
speed of sound in the air (m/s)
T
temperature (°C)
β
buffer factor - fraction of the air column that tracks ambient temperature; (1 − β) is the fraction held near breath temperature
c_breath
speed of sound at breath temperature (~35°C)
c_ambient
speed of sound at the surrounding air (indoor or outdoor)
c_eff
effective speed of sound in the column - the breath/ambient blend
c_in_eff
c_eff in the tuning room (c_ambient = c_indoor)
c_out_eff
c_eff on the field (c_ambient = c_outdoor)
shift
c_out_eff / c_in_eff - the ratio your pitch will drift by
targetHz
the pit's nominal tuning pitch (e.g., 442 Hz at room temperature)
k_pit
pit bar drift coefficient (cents per °C); negative because bars flatten in heat
T_ref
pit reference temperature (the room temp at which the bars were tuned, ~20°C)
effectiveTargetHz
the pit's actual sounding pitch on the field - what the brass should match instead of targetHz
tuneTo
the indoor target each section actually tunes to

Speed of sound depends almost entirely on temperature:

c(T) ≈ 331.3 + 0.606·T   (m/s, T in °C)

A 10°C warming raises c by about 1.8%. Because f scales with c, that same horn now plays 1.8% sharp - roughly 30 cents. Easily audible, and the entire problem this calculator pre-compensates for.

Humidity and altitude are deliberately left out. Humidity nudges the speed of sound by only a fraction of a percent, and since the calculator works from the difference between indoor and outdoor, even that mostly cancels - the net effect on a target is well under a cent. Altitude has no direct effect at all: the speed of sound depends on the ratio of air pressure to density, which stays constant as you climb. Elevation changes pitch only insofar as it changes temperature, which you are already measuring.

But the air inside a horn is not all at ambient temperature. Warm breath fills the leadpipe and the first stretch of tubing, holding it near body temperature (~35°C). The far reaches drift toward ambient as cold metal pulls heat out of the air. So the effective speed of sound is a weighted blend of two parts of the column:

c_eff = (1 − β)·c_breath + β·c_ambient

β is the fraction of the column that tracks ambient; (1 − β) is the fraction held at breath temperature. Bigger horns have higher β - more tubing far from the leadpipe, and more cold metal mass pulling things toward ambient regardless of breath input.

Apply this once for the tuning room (c_ambient = c_indoor) and once for the field (c_ambient = c_outdoor). The ratio tells you exactly how much the horn's pitch will shift between the two:

shift = c_out_eff / c_in_eff

The pit doesn't escape physics either. Bar instruments tuned at room temperature drift flat on a hot field - wood/synthetic (marimba) and aluminum (vibes) most, steel (glock) least. Since the brass needs to lock with the pit, not the nominal pitch, the calculator first shifts the target by the pit's expected drift:

effectiveTargetHz = targetHz · 2^(k_pit · (T_out − T_ref) / 1200)

k_pit is the bar drift coefficient (cents per °C, negative); T_ref is the room temperature the bars were tuned at (20°C). The "Average" option uses the mean coefficient across the three instruments.

To land on the pit's actual field pitch, tune indoors to a deliberately offset frequency that will rise by exactly the brass shift factor as the horn equilibrates:

tuneTo = effectiveTargetHz / shift

Each section gets its own tuneTo because each section has its own β. Trumpets (short tubing, breath dominates the column) get a target close to the bare effectiveTargetHz. Tubas (long tubing, lots of cold metal) get a target noticeably below it: they'll drift further sharp once the air column equilibrates outdoors, so they start lower indoors and land on the pit's pitch on the field.

What is the buffer factor?

"Buffer factor" isn't a standard acoustics term - it's a coefficient I'm using to capture a real physical effect concisely. When you play, only part of the horn's air column tracks ambient temperature; the rest sits near breath temperature because your continuous warm exhale dominates that portion. The buffer factor (β) is the fraction of the column that follows ambient.

β = 0 would mean the column is entirely at breath temperature (ambient has no effect on pitch). β = 1 would mean the column entirely tracks ambient (no breath warming at all). Real horns sit between roughly 0.65 and 0.95, scaling with instrument size - larger horns have more cold metal mass and longer tubing, so a greater fraction of the column equilibrates to ambient regardless of breath input.

A higher β means a horn's pitch shifts more for a given indoor-to-outdoor temperature change. This is why an ensemble tuned to one Hz indoors comes apart on the field: tubas (high β) drift further sharp than trumpets (low β). The calculator produces section-specific indoor targets that pre-compensate for this differential.

The defaults (0.70 / 0.75 / 0.80 / 0.90) are physics-informed estimates, not measurements of your horns. The recipe below describes how to refine them against your specific ensemble.

Why match the brass to a pit instrument?

Bar instruments in the pit are pitched by their physical bars, not by an air column, so they don't shift the same way brass does. But they do shift - bar stiffness drops slightly as temperature rises, which flattens the pitch. The size of the effect depends on the material, and (counterintuitively) is roughly comparable between aluminum and wood, with steel notably smaller:

  • Marimba (wood or synthetic) - largest measured drift, around 0.39 cents flatter per °C above room temp
  • Vibes (aluminum) - close behind, around 0.29 cents/°C
  • Glock (steel) - barely moves, around 0.05 cents/°C, but lives high enough that even small errors are exposed

The pit can't be retuned on the field, so the brass has to come to them. Pick the instrument that carries the most exposed melodic or sustained material in your book - vibes is the usual choice because its sustain makes it the most audible reference, even though marimba technically drifts more. If you're not sure, Average splits the difference.

These coefficients come from measured frequencies of front ensemble instruments at indoor (~72°F) and outdoor (~85°F) temperatures. Real bars still vary by manufacturer, age, and tuning process; refine against observation if your pit consistently sits off from where the calculator predicts.

Calibrating buffer factors for your corps

The defaults are starting points. To dial in your real numbers:

  1. For one show, set all four buffers to a single value (start with 0.85). Compute and apply the resulting target.
  2. From recordings or live observation, note how each section sat against the pit in exposed sustained moments - especially early vs. late in the show.
  3. A section drifting further sharp than predicted has a higher real β; less sharp = lower real β.
  4. Adjust the buffer values in the calculator until predicted offsets match observed.
  5. Repeat across different weather conditions over the season. Buffer factors should be stable across shows for the same instruments and players.
